## Preface and Methodology

Holliday opens by identifying two dimensions of struggle in international English language education: (1) educators needing to re-align themselves amid shifting ownership of English and culturally divisive ideologies, and (2) the difficulty of writing critically about conflict while being situated within a position of power and privilege — that of the English-speaking West and its well-resourced TESOL institutions.

The book draws on email accounts from 36 ESOL educators across 14 countries, including 20 colleagues from outside the English-speaking West, whose perspectives significantly influenced the book's direction. Holliday employs a progressivist paradigm of qualitative research centered on *thick description*, drawing on his informants' biographical and professional experiences alongside his own autobiographical narrative.

He notes a key methodological tension: he does not claim to speak *for* the TESOL people he cites, but rather speaks only for himself as a multi-cultured international professional. The informants' narratives are treated as "uniquely privileged data, grounded in biographical experience and social contexts" (citing Coffey 1999: 115).

### Acknowledgements

Holliday acknowledges 51,800 words of data from email informants and names dozens of colleagues and students who contributed to shaping the book, including researchers at Ain Shams, Damascus, and Pune Universities.

## Chapter 1: The Struggle for New Relationships

This opening chapter frames the book's central concerns: the worlds and conflicts of ESOL educators whose professional lives are both enriched and problematized by the cultural and political interfaces of working with an international language.

### ESOL Educators: The Politics of Labelling

Holliday identifies two perspectives on the TESOL profession:

1. **An inclusive We** — viewing TESOL as a single global profession of collaboration, referring to *all* teachers, academics, curriculum developers, writers, and publishers involved worldwide as "World TESOL."

2. **Political division** — recognizing that one distinct part of the TESOL world (the English-speaking West) is different from and tends to dominate the rest, described through the BANA-TESEP distinction (Holliday 1994a).

**BANA** (Britain, Australasia, and North America) comprises an innovative, predatory culture of integrated skills located in the private sector or commercially-run language centres. **TESEP** (Tertiary, Secondary, Primary) comprises a more traditional culture of academic subjects located in state education systems worldwide.

Holliday notes that this professional-cultural distinction is often lost in the way terms are used within the TESEP culture, and that the vast majority of people who work within TESEP come from outside the English-speaking West.

### 'Native' and 'Non-Native Speakers'

The chapter examines the contested terminology around 'native' and 'non-native speakers.' Holliday places these terms in inverted commas throughout to signal that they are disputed. He notes Jenkins's (2000: 8–9) argument for the term 'non-native speaker' being "anachronistic," and Braine's (1999: xv) observation that 'native speakers' themselves "do not speak the idealized, standardized version of their language."

Multiple email informants share their complex experiences with these labels. One informant, Esmat, observes:

> I believe that English is owned by the speaker (native or otherwise). I do not consider myself a native speaker but I consider myself bilingual. (email interview)

### English as an International Language

The ideology of native-speakerism is based on the assumption that 'native speakers' of English have a special claim to the language itself. But as Graddol (1997) and others demonstrate, the majority use of English is now outside the English-speaking West. Widdowson (1998) makes the point:

> How English develops in the world is no business whatever of native speakers in England, the United States or anywhere else.... The very fact that English is an international language means that no nation can have custody over it. (Widdowson 1998: 244–5)

### Native-Speakerism

Holliday defines native-speakerism as "an established belief that 'native-speaker' teachers represent a 'Western culture' from which spring the ideals of both the English language and of English language teaching methodology." He traces how this ideology spreads beyond geographical origin through professional discourses and socialization.

### Two Positions

Holliday introduces a framework of two contrasting positions:

**Table 1.1: From Native-Speakerism to Position 2**

| | Native-Speakerism | Position 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Orientation** | Traditional, 'us'-'them' position. Setting superior 'native speakers' against inferior 'non-native speakers'. Cultural difference. | Struggle to make new relationships. 'We are all in this together.' Cultural continuity. |
| **Language and context** | English is foreign. The 'native speaker' is the norm. | English is international. The local context is the norm. |
| **ESOL educator from outside the English-speaking West** | She is a 'non-native speaker'. English is someone else's, with a foreign culture and expertise. There is an instrumental use of foreign expertise. | She is a teacher at home. English is hers. She has her own ways of doing things. She can also be a teacher 'abroad', like any other teacher. |
| **ESOL educator from the English-speaking West** | She is a 'native speaker'. She brings her English and culture variety—but perhaps parochial. She has theories of exotic foreign cultures. | She is a speaker of a dominant English variety—but perhaps parochial. She needs to rethink the normality of what she brings, and what she meets. |

### 'Non-Native Speaker' Identity and Ownership

Holliday reports how informants from outside the English-speaking West demonstrate growing confidence in the development of a profession not dominated by 'native speakers.' Fauzia Shamim, a Pakistani academic, notes:

> Competent non-native teachers and teacher educators who are also proficient in the language (whatever the variety of English they may be using) are beginning to question the relevance of the ideas imported from the centre. (email interview)

### Problematizing 'Native Speaker' Dominance

An important implication of Position 2 is that English-speaking Western colleagues must rethink and problematize their entire role. Vandrick (2002a) deconstructs her relations with childhood playmates in India as the daughter of a missionary, exploring how "colonial-like influences" unconsciously affect attitudes.

### The Sites of Struggle

The chapter concludes by identifying three areas of struggle in World TESOL:
1. The changing status of English as an international language
2. The existence of native-speakerism dividing World TESOL
3. The responsibility of English-speaking Western colleagues to deal with professional culture and native-speakerism

## Chapter 2: Culturist Perceptions of 'Us' and 'Them'

This chapter takes up the issue of culturism — the tendency within ESOL to reduce the foreign Other to an essentialist stereotype — and explores movement from native-speakerism toward a more inclusive Position 2.

### The Constituents of Culturism

Holliday uses the term 'culturism' to describe "any thought or act which reduces a person to something less than what she is according to an essentialist view of culture." Culturism belongs to a wider set of chauvinism which includes sexism and racism.

### Essentialism

Essentialism presumes that "particular things have essences which serve to identify them as the particular things that they are" (Bullock and Trombley 1997: 283). In the context of culture, this leads to viewing nations, regions, and continents as containers of "mutually exclusive types of behaviour" so that people are "essentially different" based on where they are 'from.'

### Reification

Holliday explains reification as a process of 'making real' (Berger and Luckmann 1979: 107), whereby the products of human activity are conceptualized *as if* they were natural or divine. Through reification, ideology and culture become fixed and ordinary in people's minds. He summarizes:

> essentialist + colonialist + politics of + reification = Culturism
> view of      ideology        Self and
> culture                      Other

### A Non-Essentialist View

In opposition to essentialism, Holliday proposes a non-essentialist view where culture is not a geographical place but "a social force, which is evident wherever it emerges as being significant" (Roberts *et al.* 2001: 54). Culture flows and shifts between us in different ways at different times and circumstances.

A non-essentialist, small culture approach considers any instance of socially cohesive behavior as culture — from football teams to restaurants to professional cultures such as TESOL.

### A Generalized Other

The chapter introduces the concept of the *generalized Other* — a negative construction rooted in colonialist and essentialist ideology. The Other is constructed as opposite to the familiar Self, with "often falsely attributed negative or exotic characteristics which are opposite to the positive characteristics of the Self" (Pennycook 1998: 10–16).

Holliday provides a detailed listing of stereotypical characteristics:

**Unproblematic Self** ('native speaker' students and colleagues):
- Independent, autonomous, creative, original, individualist
- Analytical, objective, flexible, critical, negotiating knowledge
- Able to manage, research, plan, evaluate, organize, train, make decisions
- Involved in genuine teacher–student interaction

**Culturally Problematic Other** ('non-native speaker' students and colleagues):
- Dependent, hierarchical, collectivist, reticent, indirect, passive, docile, lacking in self-esteem
- Uncritical, static, rigid, with a fixed view of knowledge, intellectually interdependent, wishing to preserve knowledge, good at memorizing
- Need to be trained, treated sensitively, understood, involved, given ownership, empowered
- Prefer frontal teaching, exam-oriented; find decision-making difficult
- Reluctant to challenge authority, easily dominated, undemocratic
- Traditional, Confucian, Islamic, etc.

### Culturism in TESOL

The chapter presents a detailed five-step "culturist methodology" observed at an international TESOL conference:

1. See an individual's behaviour as mostly explainable by membership of a foreign national culture
2. Describe the foreign national culture as a generalized Other, whose main characteristic is difference to Self
3. Find the details of this difference
4. Explain all behaviour in terms of this difference
5. Reify the stereotype

### A Culturist Methodology

Evidence from conference presentations and research writing demonstrates how these steps operate in practice, with an example from a conference presentation about teachers in an East Asian country Holliday calls 'Ex.'

### Excavating the Strata of Culturism

Holliday provides examples of how culturism operates unconsciously in professional discourse, including:
- Grace's research on Chinese university TESOL containing culturist descriptions of student behavior
- His own lesson observation notes from Hong Kong, where he realizes the ideological implications of his own assumptions

### The Politics of Professionalism

The chapter examines how culturism develops within professional groups through exclusivity, the superiority of specialized knowledge, and Foucauldian analysis of how professions appropriate fields of expertise as exclusive territory.

### Gaining Academic and Professional Capital Abroad

English-speaking Western TESOL educators gain professional status through establishing academic and professional capital in research, methodology, and territory. Despite excellent teaching resources, teachers in Britain and North America often suffer from lack of status, part-time transient employment, and low earning power.

### 'Native Speakers' Abroad

'Native speaker' status and the professional knowledge and cultural superiority associated with it become meaningful in other countries, serving as a basis for international recognition of English-speaking Western institutions as centres of excellence.

### Complex Identities

Through Aliya's biographical account, the chapter illustrates the complex way native-speakerism cuts across categories. Aliya, a Pakistani academic who spent most of her professional life in the UAE, defies easy categorization as either 'native' or 'non-native speaker.'

### Not a Simple Matter

Holliday concludes that the 'us'-'them' threads of native-speakerism are deeply woven into culturist chauvinism that pervades late modern society, has roots in a colonialist past, and requires all World TESOL professionals to "digest the normal complexity of the lives of those who are different from us."

## Chapter 3: The Legacy of Lockstep

This chapter explores the conditions within which native-speakerism has developed as a powerful force in World TESOL, focusing on major icons of native-speakerist practice: 'the four skills,' close monitoring, staged teaching, and oral elicitation. Holliday traces these back to their origins in audiolingualism.

### A Moving, Adapting Practice

Through Robert's account of his teaching experience in Hong Kong, Holliday illustrates how English-speaking Western TESOL moves, adapts, and establishes itself in other locations. Robert's practice was a complex mixture of British state education influences, sensitivity to local context, and preservation of certain professional elements.

### Cultural Icons

Holliday defines 'cultural icons' as "social concepts that are venerated by a particular cultural group and which, in the case of English-speaking Western TESOL, are sustained." He uses the framework of small culture formation (Figure 3.1) to show how icons operate through:
- (i) Need for group cohesion: recipe, convention, discourse, identity, expression, exclusivity
- (ii) Cultural residues and influences: family, nation, region, peers, profession, institution
- (iii) Social constructions: routinization, institutionalization, naturalization
- (iv) Products: artefacts, art, literature, values, discourse of and about 'culture,' ideology

### 'The Four Skills' as a Cultural Icon

'The four skills' (listening, speaking, reading, and writing) remain a powerful ideological force of staging and control in English-speaking Western TESOL. Hedge (2000) devotes 147 out of 400 pages of her reference book *Teaching and Learning in the Language Classroom* to the teaching of the four skills. Harmer (2001) similarly devotes 79 out of 365 pages (almost 25%) to 'the four skills' in *The Practice of English Language Teaching*.

### Table 3.1: The Sustainability of Icons

This table plots the sustained trajectory of cultural icons from audiolingualism (column A) through the early communicative approach (column B) to the later communicative approach/weak version (column C), showing how elements such as oral-first emphasis, mastery of prescribed oracy, four skills, structures, lockstep, PPP, realia, and elicitation have persisted and evolved across these paradigm shifts.

### Staged Learning

The oral-first preoccupation has shaped how students are perceived in terms of participation and classroom activity. The tight behaviourist sequencing required that students (1) listen, *then* (2) speak what they have heard, *then* (3) read what they have heard and spoken, and only *then* (4) write what they have heard, spoken, and read.

### A Modernistic Discourse

Holliday identifies key iconic phrases that cluster to form the dominant discourse:
- *Learner-centredness*, *learner autonomy*, and *authenticity*
- *Genuine language*

Associated with these are two discoursal clusters:
- Accountable learning: training, needs, objectives, 'the four skills,' competences
- Oral expression: initiation, self-direction, groups and pairs

### The Residues of Audiolingualism

Small culture formation produces ideology, and native-speakerism is one product of English-speaking Western TESOL's dominant culture. The chapter traces how the modernistic focus on oral language skills has been translated into highly organized behavioural training to correct the 'culture' of the foreign Other.

### Beginning with Audiolingualism

The term 'audiolingualism' refers to basic methodological ideas common in the US and Britain from the 1950s until the communicative revolution in the mid-1970s. The American audiolingual method, derived from the structural approach, "followed the orthodox 'four skills'" and "required a considerable amount of aural-oral drill work" (Howatt 1984: 225).

### Corrective Training

The residues of elicitation in classroom practice relate to the notion of 'learner-training,' rooted in the need to train one's students "as the members of an orchestra, to respond precisely and instantly to the signs and symbols within the elicitation repertoire of the audiolingual teacher."

### Figure 3.2: Confrontational Lesson

This figure depicts the U-shaped classroom arrangement characteristic of the English-speaking Western TESOL 'learning group ideal,' showing a standing, mobile teacher in the center surrounded by seated students in a *high-surveillance* classroom where "the teacher is on top of the students monitoring every move and utterance."

### Training in Corrective Training

Baxter (2003) demonstrates how the ethos of corrective training is reproduced in teacher training, where "the teacher trainer 'employs the same methods she is trying to inculcate in the trainees to teach them'" and "many strategies used were 'taken directly from the language classroom.'"

### Corrective Surveillance

Having predetermined behaviour in ostensibly discovery-oriented training sessions reveals "a basic contradiction which I see inherent of the so-called communicative break from the lockstep of audiolingualism and the ambivalence of the apparently liberal but in effect deeply controlling panopticon."

Foucault (1991: 202) describes the panopticon as a system which:

> automatizes and disindividualizes power.... There is a machinery that assures dissymmetry, disequilibrium, difference. Consequently it does not matter who exercises power. Any individual, taken almost at random, can operate the machine.

### Control and Transportable Professional Confidence

Holliday reflects on his own experience, noting how the professional confidence gained from audiolingualism — "the most up-to-date, systematic method for teaching English efficiently" — gave young teachers the certainty to enter foreign professional contexts. He traces how this confidence was sustained through the technicalization of English for specific purposes and remained central to the profession's sense of its own authority.

## Chapter 4: 'Learner-Centredness' and 'Autonomy'

This chapter examines how two key concepts of English-speaking Western TESOL professionalism — learner-centredness and autonomy — promote native-speakerism by governing how 'we' (native speakers) package 'them' ('non-native speaker' students) through a teacher-led process of control-construction, attempting to correct their cultures.

### Learner-Centredness

Anderson (2003a: 247) observes that:

> Learner-centredness was, for the teachers, a means to rationalize the TESOL ideal as a whole. In a sense, it was the principal driving force of the ideal.... It can therefore be argued that the teachers' construction of learner-centredness reveals much about their construction of the TESOL ideal as a whole.

### The Original Ideal

Position 2 holds that language learning is owned by the student — a realization that gave rise to the communicative break from audiolingualism and the rise of learner-centredness in the 1970s. However, native-speakerism has hijacked learner-centredness and pushed it in the opposite direction.

### Table 4.1: Arguments for Learner-Centredness

This table compares the TESOL (psycholinguistic) and Education (sociological) approaches to learner-centredness across several dimensions:
- **Approach**: TESOL derives from second language acquisition hypotheses; Education treats knowledge as an open system and resource
- **Critique of teacher-frontedness**: TESOL focuses on teacher–student interaction being conventionalized; Education notes lack of respect shown by teachers
- **What needs to be changed**: TESOL focuses on student needs and learning styles; Education seeks clarity about skills and information to be learnt
- **The value of group work**: TESOL emphasizes equal share of classroom talk; Education capitalizes on the social psychology of small groups

### Bureaucratization and Technicalization in Practice

Holliday examines how the concept of 'learner-centredness' has been transformed into a bureaucratic, technicalized system that paradoxically *increases* teacher control. The chapter traces how needs analysis, individual learning plans, and learning contracts become instruments of surveillance and control.

### Autonomy, Native-Speakerism, and Culturism

The discussion of autonomy within English-speaking Western TESOL is critiqued for its culturist assumptions — that 'non-native speaker' students from certain cultures lack the capacity for autonomous learning and need to be trained toward it.

### The Need to See Beyond the Ideology

Holliday argues for seeing beyond the ideological constraints of native-speakerism within learner-centredness and autonomy, recognizing that these concepts have been "hijacked" to serve a controlling professional agenda.

## Chapter 5: Social Autonomy and Authenticity

This chapter explores the concepts of social autonomy and authenticity, arguing that genuine autonomy exists outside the controlled environments of English-speaking Western TESOL classrooms.

### Classroom Regime as Inhibitor

The chapter examines how the English-speaking Western TESOL classroom regime can actually *inhibit* genuine autonomy and authentic language use, despite claiming to promote them.

### Resistance

Holliday draws on examples from Hong Kong, Sri Lanka, and other contexts to show how students and teachers resist the impositions of the dominant TESOL methodology. This resistance takes various forms — from subtle non-compliance to the development of alternative practices. Research from Tong (1996) on Hong Kong secondary school students and Canagarajah (1999) on Sri Lankan students demonstrates that "the students find the dominant culture of the classroom dangerous and take action to deal with it in private sites."

### Social Authenticity

Against the narrow TESOL concept of 'authentic materials,' Holliday proposes a broader notion of *social authenticity* rooted in the real communicative practices and social realities of learners' lives.

### An Opening Up, and an Undoing of Prejudice

The chapter concludes by suggesting that recognizing social autonomy and authenticity opens up possibilities for genuine engagement between TESOL educators and students from diverse cultural backgrounds, moving toward an undoing of the prejudices embedded in native-speakerist thinking.

## Chapter 6: 'Stakeholder-Centredness'

This chapter examines curriculum development projects and their relationship to native-speakerism, focusing on how stakeholder-centredness can either reinforce or challenge the dominant ideology.

### Projects and People

Holliday reviews major English language teaching projects and their impact on local educational contexts, examining how the involvement of 'native speaker' experts often marginalizes local stakeholders. He draws on examples from Damascus University, Ain Shams University, and other international contexts.

### Writing the Stakeholders In

The concept of stakeholder-centredness is proposed as an alternative to top-down curriculum development, emphasizing the need to include all parties in the educational process — students, teachers, programme designers, managers, and administrators.

### Devaluing Other Realities

The chapter critiques how English-speaking Western TESOL projects systematically devalue the educational realities and expertise of local educators, treating local contexts as deficient rather than different.

### Bucking the Imperialism Thesis

While acknowledging the importance of Phillipson's (1992) linguistic imperialism thesis, Holliday nuances the discussion by showing that the relationship between 'Centre' and 'Periphery' is more complex than a simple domination model. He examines cases where local educators have successfully resisted or adapted Western TESOL approaches.

### Figure 6.1: Insiders and Outsiders Switch Roles

This figure illustrates how the positions of insiders and outsiders can shift and reverse in different professional and educational contexts, challenging fixed notions of who holds authority and expertise.

### Seeing Individual Richness

The chapter concludes by advocating for recognition of the individual richness and complexity of people in all educational contexts, moving beyond stereotypical categories.

## Chapter 7: Critiquing Appropriate Methodology

This chapter examines the concept of 'appropriate methodology' and its relationship to native-speakerism, evaluating both its potential and its limitations.

### Separating the Positive from the Negative

Holliday argues for distinguishing between the positive potential of appropriate methodology (genuinely responding to local needs) and its negative manifestations (another form of Western TESOL control).

### Figure 7.1: TESOL and Social Context

This figure depicts the relationship between TESOL practice and social context, showing how methodology is embedded within broader social, cultural, and political structures.

### 'Our' System or People

The chapter examines a fundamental tension: is appropriate methodology about adapting 'our' (Western) system to fit other contexts, or about genuinely engaging with the people in those contexts?

### Table 7.1: Parameters for Critiquing Appropriate Methodology

This table presents a framework with five rows and multiple columns, organized around key parameters:

| | Row 1 (left): Native-speakerism | Row 1 (right): Position 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Row 1** | 'Our' system or people: Apply our methodology to their context | Stakeholder-centredness: Take heed of all relevant parties |
| **Row 2** | Manipulate their culture to fit curriculum | Adapt to accommodate 'dynamic, open-ended, self-correcting' system |
| **Row 3** | Ecological approach: Investigate local features for the purpose of effecting the well-being of the culture | Collaborative inclusivity |
| **Row 4** | Communicating with local exigencies | Exploratory research and learning in dialogue with the environment |
| **Row 5** | Outcome of native-speakerism | Cultural continuity |

### Communicating with the Ecology

Holliday discusses Bax's (2003a, 2003b) proposal for a 'context approach,' noting how it touches on similar issues as Canagarajah but from a different direction. While Canagarajah speaks from the critical, political, postmodern camp, Bax speaks from the instrumental, modernist camp, both commenting on the pervasive obsession with communicative language teaching.

### Dissecting the Culture

Holliday examines how the ecological approach, while valuable, can still fall into culturist patterns when it treats local cultures as problems to be solved rather than realities to be respected.

### Lack of Collaboration

The chapter critiques the lack of genuine collaboration in many TESOL projects, where local colleagues are "investigated as ignorant subjects rather than as collaborating colleagues."

### Local Researchers

To see how far the investigative side of appropriate methodology can overcome these criticisms, Holliday examines cases of educators from outside the English-speaking West — particularly Azer at Ain Shams University in Egypt, who applied a cultural engineering approach "extremely well" by:
1. Stretching his very large grammar class (over 350 students) to accommodate extensive workshop activities as well as lectures
2. Maintaining social distance between the lecturer and students constructively
3. Capitalizing on cultural elements outside the classroom such as the students' propensity for autonomous collaboration

### Emergent Practices

Holliday reports two examples suggesting that communicative principles can operate independently of English-speaking Western TESOL construction:
1. A lesson observed in a Chinese university (Holliday 1997a) where the teacher fulfills all three communicative principles with "tremendous precision and analysis"
2. Jacob's (1991) description of 'communicative' teaching carried out in lectures by Indian university colleagues

### The Puzzle of Position 2

The chapter concludes by acknowledging that achieving Position 2 — a genuinely inclusive approach — is extremely difficult within the existing structures of English-speaking Western TESOL discourse.

## Chapter 8: The Struggle for Cultural Continuity

In this final chapter, Holliday considers cultural continuity as a solution to native-speakerism. Cultural continuity is presented in contrast to a native-speakerist ethos of cultural difference, offering a basis on which all ESOL educators can be equally represented and valued.

### Correction vs. Understanding

Native-speakerism and Position 2 are differentiated by ways of *seeing* and *making sense*, underpinned by different motivations for knowledge:
- **Position 2** represents a desire to know and understand who and how we are so that we can achieve *cultural continuity* by living and working together without discrimination
- **Native-speakerism** represents a desire to know about a foreign Other in order to change and 'improve' it, driven by *cultural correction*

This distinction echoes Said's (2003) differentiation between two types of knowledge:

> But there is a difference between knowledge of other peoples and other times that is the result of understanding, compassion, careful study and analysis... and knowledge that is part of an overall campaign of self-affirmation. There is, after all, a profound difference between the will to understand for purposes of co-existence and enlargement of horizons, and the will to dominate for the purposes of control.

### Dealing with Professional Division

The life data from email informants expresses the ongoing struggle with divisions created by native-speakerism as a movement toward cultural continuity. Holliday identifies three groups engaged in this struggle:
- Teachers outside the English-speaking West dealing with the 'non-native speaker' label and asserting identity, professional status, and employability
- English-speaking Western ESOL educators fighting their prejudices, learning to appreciate the meanings their students bring, and dealing with the changing global ownership of English
- Language students and teachers dealing with the cultural dilemmas implicit in language learning

### Changing Professional Image

A major barrier to cultural continuity is the division between so-called 'native speaker' and 'non-native speaker' educators. Informants suggest this could be aided by changing the professional image in schools and universities, gradually replacing 'native speakers' with 'non-native speakers' in positions traditionally reserved for native speakers only.

### Changing Curricula

Deliberate action is needed to encourage students to appreciate cultural continuity within their own and their teachers' positions in the process of English education. This requires changes in curriculum to undo what one informant, Aliya, calls "the 'charisma' of the West."

### Addressing Deeper Political Issues

Cultural continuity cannot be achieved simply by removing professional divisions. The suggestions in this section need to be seen within a deeper political context of long-standing post-colonialist dominant discourse, particularly in the wake of 11 September 2001 and the subsequent renewal of cultural divisions.

### Renewed Entrenchments

Holliday examines how the post-9/11 political climate has created new barriers to cultural continuity, with several informants expressing concern about deepening cultural polarization.

### The Persistence of Colonial Structures

The chapter traces how the historical legacy of how English "should be taught" has persisted through British government and British Council influence, perpetuating the notion that English ought to be taught as a domain where "they are experts in."

Sullivan (2000) offers a sobering observation about the 'ownership of English':

> The Centre is not more critical of its practices in terms of the service that it needs to offer to English language learners, but more for the control that it seems to be losing with the changing of ownership of the language and subsequent loss of authority over 'how things should be done'. (email interview)

### Understanding Other Agendas

English and its methodologies need to be put in their place — as an instrumental, though very rich resource, "which does not *have* to represent the cultural values of the English-speaking West, but which can absorb and express the depths of whatever cultural values with which it becomes associated."

### Working from the Inside Out

Approaches to cultural continuity which avoid domination by the English-speaking West must develop counter to their self-affirming dominant discourses. Holliday examines Jacob's (1996) work as an Indian curriculum project coordinator at Pune University, describing a process of "counteracting imperialism through a conscious *resistance discourse* that is created in the context of a collaborative model."

### Table 8.1: Intermediate Actions of Resistance

| Strategies | Applications by Rustam and colleagues in University X | Applications by Hong Kong students |
|---|---|---|
| **Marking territories** | Establishing an evaluation scheme meaningful to local colleagues; establishing significant events such as seminars | Establishing their own spaces in classrooms, lecture theatres, and workrooms |
| **Reconciling with the past** | Making sure the schema is rooted in and continues to grow from existing cultures | Making connections with previous experience in community, art, and education |
| **Speaking against the grain** | Speaking out against Guhaz's and Gabby's representation of the matrix; establishing anti-matrix ideas in reports and presentations | Overturning plans for project work agreed with tutors |
| **Moving to centre stage from the periphery** | Institutional actions as above | Designing and presenting research projects in their own terms |
| **Holding back technology** | Resisting the timelines, documents, and hierarchies of the matrix; resisting Gabby's imported evaluation scheme | Refusing to speak in formal classes |
| **Breaking silences** | Speaking out as above | Speaking out when it is necessary to resist |

### Penetrating Worlds of Behaviour

Jacob (1996) describes a small *counterculture* — "a small culture which asserts itself against a dominant small culture or cultures, but one which, in order to achieve continuity, tries to make sense of the dominant culture in different ways in order to survive and make itself known."

### Bringing Worlds Together

The irony is that the intermediate actions of resistance and the enacting of social autonomy in the counterculture are more similar to what the dominant culture is seeking than can be imagined. "It ought to be the role of the teacher or the curriculum developer to *work with* the counterculture, and to help cultural continuity along by being instrumental in bringing the worlds of the dominant and counter cultures together."

### A More Neutral Geography

At the curriculum level, cultural continuity is encouraged by a small culture approach because it encourages "an equality of status for all concerned parties, with no a priori primacy given to an imported English-speaking Western TESOL culture."

### The Interpersonal and the Global

The discussion leads in two apparently conflicting directions: Holliday suggests 18 disciplines for making and seeing each other as individuals who are culturally constructed in complex ways rather than bound by stereotypes:

> Seek a deeper understanding of individual people's identity by avoiding preconceptions, appreciating complexity, not over-generalizing from individual instances. Achieve this by employing *bracketing* to put aside your preconceptions, *thick description* to enable you to see complexity, and an appreciation of *emergent data* to signal the unexpected.... Seek a deeper understanding of the prejudices, pre-occupations and discourses which lead you to otherize. Use this to enable *bracketing* and to manage your own role in communication.... Seek a deeper understanding of the representations of the foreign Other which are perpetuated by society.
> (Holliday *et al.* 2004: 48–9)

### Self-Understanding and Discipline

The issue of cultural essentialism — the basis for culturism — can be addressed at the interpersonal level through a vision of cultural continuity as interpersonal relations within a melange of small cultures.

### Sociological Imagination

To place cultural continuity within a wider, global scenario, it is necessary to achieve what Mills (1970) calls a 'sociological imagination' — the ability to locate oneself and one's actions critically within a world scenario, "to grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society."

### Loose Ends and Enriching Encounters

The chapter and book conclude with a reflection on the inevitably open-ended nature of the discussion. Holliday invokes Geertz:

> Coherence cannot be the major test of validity for a cultural description.... There is nothing so coherent as a paranoid's delusion or a swindler's story. The force of our interpretations cannot rest on the tightness with which they hold together, or the assurance with which they are argued. Nothing has done more, I think, to discredit cultural analysis than the impeccable depictions of formal order in whose actual existence nobody can quite believe.
> (Geertz 1993: 17–18)

The book ends with a hopeful image of intercultural encounter:

> If the *final* thought is that she is not 'foreign' then we are lost.
